A brief history of computing

A brief history of computing

The Antikythera mechanism

Babbage & Lovelace engine

Images: Wikimedia Commons — Antikythera (CC BY-SA 3.0), Difference Engine (public domain)

  • Antikythera mechanism: geared bronze device from ancient Greece, c. 150–100 BC, recovered from a shipwreck in 1901. An analogue computer for predicting astronomical positions and eclipses. Nothing of comparable complexity survives for over a millennium afterwards.
  • Difference Engine: designed by Charles Babbage in the 1820s to compute polynomial tables by the method of finite differences — mechanising the work of human “computers”. This is the 1853 engraving; a full engine wasn’t built until the Science Museum’s working construction in 1991.
  • Ada Lovelace: worked on Babbage’s follow-up design, the Analytical Engine (general-purpose: conditional branching, loops, memory). Her 1843 notes on it contain what’s often called the first published program (computing Bernoulli numbers) — and the deeper insight that such a machine could manipulate any symbols, not just numbers: computation beyond arithmetic.

Douglas Hartree and his differential analyser

Meccano differential analyser for computing atomic spectra

\(1\,E_\mathrm{h} = \dfrac{\hbar^2}{m_\mathrm{e} a_0^2} = \dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a_0} \approx 27.2\ \mathrm{eV}\)

Images: differential analyser (Sylvain Machefert, CC BY-SA 4.0); portrait (fair use)

  • Douglas Hartree (1897–1958): Manchester physicist, of Hartree–Fock / self-consistent-field fame — atomic structure calculations by hand, and one of the first to see machines as the way forward.
  • With Arthur Porter he built a differential analyser in the mid-1930s — the first model famously out of Meccano — solving differential equations by mechanical integration: analogue, continuous, purpose-built.
  • War story: my own run-in with unit systems in a DFT code. The Coulomb constant \(e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0\) is exactly 1 in Hartree atomic units (1 Ha·\(a_0\)), but in Rydberg × Ångström units it’s \(2 \times 0.529177 = 1.058\) — the factor of 2 (Ry→Ha) and the 0.529 (\(a_0\)→Å) nearly cancel, so mixing conventions gives answers that are only ~6% off: far nastier to spot than a factor of 2. Moral: “atomic units” is ambiguous; know your code’s conventions.

“The implications of the machine are so vast that we cannot conceive how they will affect our civilisation. Here you have something which is making one field of human activity 1,000 times faster. In the field of transportation, the equivalent to ACE would be the ability to travel from London to Cambridge … in five seconds as a regular thing. It is almost unimaginable.”

— Douglas Hartree, on the Automatic Computing Engine, 1946

…and yet, all the calculations that would ever be needed in this country could be done on three digital computers: one in Cambridge, one in Teddington, and one in Manchester.

— Hartree in 1951, as recalled by Lord Bowden

  • First quote: Hartree in the press coverage of NPL’s announcement of the ACE (Automatic Computing Engine, Turing’s design), 1946.
  • Second: paraphrased — Lord Bowden’s recollection of Hartree’s advice in 1951: the three machines then being built (EDSAC in Cambridge, Pilot ACE at NPL Teddington, the Mark 1 in Manchester) would suffice for the country; “no one else would ever need machines of their own, or would be able to afford to buy them.”
  • Same man, five years apart: even those who saw the revolution coming couldn’t imagine its scale.

The Manchester “Baby” (1948)

EDSAC, Cambridge (1949)

Images: Manchester Baby replica (Logg Tandy, CC BY 4.0); EDSAC (University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory, CC BY 2.0)

The first stored-program computers - the birth of software?

  • The Manchester “Baby” (Small-Scale Experimental Machine): ran the world’s first stored program on 21 June 1948, at the University of Manchester — program and data both held in (Williams-tube) memory, the model every modern computer follows. This is the working replica at the Science and Industry Museum, Manchester.
  • EDSAC (Cambridge, first ran May 1949, Maurice Wilkes): the first stored-program machine run as a service for scientists — early users included crystallographers and theoretical chemists. Baby proved the concept; EDSAC did science with it.

The first actual computer bug (Harvard Mark II, 1947)

Image: Wikimedia Commons (public domain, US Navy)

  • 9 September 1947, Harvard Mark II: operators traced a fault to a moth in relay #70, panel F, and taped it into the logbook — “First actual case of bug being found”. The page is now in the Smithsonian; Grace Hopper made the story famous.
  • The joke in “first actual case”: “bug” for a mechanical defect was already old engineering slang (Edison used it) — this one just happened to be literal.
  • Read the same log page: the “antan” (arctangent) test starts at 08:00 and stops at 10:00 — two hours to check arctan values. Worth pausing on: that’s the kind of work these machines existed for.

Parts of the first four US Army computers (ENIAC, EDVAC, ORDVAC, BRLESC), 1945–1962

BRLESC-I in full

Images: Wikimedia Commons — Army computers, BRLESC-I (both public domain, US Army)

  • 1962 US Army photo: each woman holds a component of equivalent computing capability from successive Ballistic Research Laboratory machines — ENIAC (1946), EDVAC, ORDVAC, and BRLESC-I — showing the downsizing of hardware from vacuum tubes towards transistors.
  • The far-right board belongs to BRLESC-I (1962), shown in full on the next reveal: board-sized capability, still a room-sized machine.
  • Women in computing: “computer” was a job title before it was a machine — and at BRL it was rooms of women computing ballistics tables by hand and desk calculator. When ENIAC replaced them, its first programmers were six of those women (Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, Ruth Lichterman) — there were no manuals; they worked it out from the wiring diagrams. At the 1946 press unveiling they went unnamed, and their role was only widely recognised decades later.
  • Early programming was often seen as low-status “women’s work” (the hardware was the prestigious part); the profession masculinised as its status rose. Grace Hopper (compilers, COBOL) is the other name to drop here.

In the meantime… hard disks, programming languages, operating systems, parallel computers, graphics, personal computers, networking, the Internet, the World Wide Web

Images: Wikimedia Commons — first transistor (public domain), Intel 4004 (Thomas Nguyen, CC BY-SA 4.0), Apple M1 (CC0)

  • 1947, Bell Labs: Bardeen, Brattain (and Shockley) — the point-contact transistor; this is the replica of the germanium-and-gold original. Solid state: no vacuum tubes, no heat, nothing to wear out — the thing that shrinks.
  • 1971, Intel 4004: the first commercial microprocessor — a whole CPU on one chip, 2,300 transistors, originally built for a Japanese desk calculator.
  • 2020, Apple M1: 16 billion transistors in a few square centimetres — ten orders of magnitude in 73 years. That exponential (Moore’s law) is the economic fact that shaped modern computing.

What have computers been used for?

  • First, science — tables, trajectories, atomic structure: machines built by and for researchers
  • Then, the military: ballistics, cryptography, bomb guidance
  • Then, commerce: payroll, banking, inventory
  • Then, suddenly, everyone: PC revolution + the internet/web democratised computing: communication, creativity, everyday life
  • Yet the frontier is still pushed by science and nowadays by AI
  • Science first: EDSAC ran as a service for Cambridge researchers; crystallography and quantum chemistry were early customers.
  • Military: ENIAC’s ballistics tables, Bletchley’s Colossus, the H-bomb calculations that ate early machine time.
  • Commerce: LEO (1951) — the world’s first business computer, built by J. Lyons & Co., a tea shop chain, for payroll and bakery logistics.
  • Democratisation: the web was invented at CERN, by science, for science — then swallowed the world.
  • Frontier: exascale simulation, AlphaFold, large language models — the biggest machines are once again doing science (and training AI).

LUMI, Kajaani, Finland (2022)

MareNostrum 4, Barcelona (2017)

Images: LUMI (LUMI consortium / Fade Creative); MareNostrum 4 (Gemmaribasmaspoch, CC BY-SA 4.0)

  • LUMI: EuroHPC’s pre-exascale machine, AMD GPUs, living in a converted paper mill in Kajaani — run on hydroelectric power, with its waste heat feeding the town’s district heating. Science still fills rooms; the rooms just got greener.
  • MareNostrum 4: Barcelona Supercomputing Center, installed in the deconsecrated Torre Girona chapel — routinely called the world’s most beautiful data centre.
  • Callback to BRLESC: seventy years on, the frontier machine is still a room — but now it’s identical racks of the same chips as everyone else’s, and the differentiator is the interconnect between them.

Software in research today

  • Simulations: molecular dynamics, quantum chemistry and materials science, climate, weather, astrophysics, complex systems
  • Automation: instrument control, robotics, high-throughput experiments, laboratory information management systems
  • Data analysis: large-scale experiments, imaging, plotting

Computing architecture

What is the machine actually doing?

Intel 8008 (1972), die labelled

  • The CPU receives instructions as “machine code”, human-readable as assembly
  • decodes each one
  • and executes it, shuttling values between the registers (right) and the arithmetic/logic unit (left)
  • All chip design since revolves around efficiently cramming as much compute as possible into this layout: specialised registers, specialised instructions…
  • …and keeping it fed: modern CPUs pipeline instructions, perform branch prediction execute speculatively and out of order, and hide slow memory behind layers of caches

Image: Ken Shirriff, righto.com — used with attribution

  • The whole fetch–decode–execute cycle is visible on this die: the instruction decoder, the registers, the ALU, the scratchpad — every box on the label is a physical block of transistors.
  • 8008 (1972): Intel’s first 8-bit microprocessor, ~3,500 transistors — simple enough that one person (Ken Shirriff) can reverse-engineer it from photographs.
  • Everything a modern CPU does is this, plus 50 years of tricks (caches, pipelines, speculation) — the mental model stays valid.
  • The tricks leak, too: branch prediction is why sorted data can be faster to process than unsorted; speculative execution gave us the Spectre/Meltdown security holes. Fetch–decode–execute is still the truth underneath.

Instruction sets: x86 and ARM

  • x86: the Intel 8086 (1978) instruction set — descendant of the 8008, still running nearly every “Intel or AMD” machine
  • amd64 / x86-64: its 64-bit extension — designed by AMD (2003), adopted by Intel, extended to include vectorisation to make the most of wider registers (SSE, AVX)
  • ARM (Acorn Computers, Cambridge, 1985): the only real competitor — every phone, Apple M-series, now Windows laptops

  • Backwards compatibility is the moat: a modern x86 CPU still boots in 8086-compatible “real mode” — 45+ years of old binaries keep running, which is why the architecture survives despite its baggage.
  • Itanium (IA-64) was Intel’s clean-slate 64-bit bet; it flopped, AMD’s x86-64 won, and the names stuck: amd64 = x86-64 = x64 — all the same thing.
  • ARM = originally “Acorn RISC Machine” (BBC Micro lineage). RISC: fewer, simpler instructions — easier to make low-power. ARM sells designs; Apple, Qualcomm, etc. build the chips.
  • Practical hook for students: this is why download pages offer x86_64/amd64 vs arm64/aarch64 builds — pick the wrong one and it won’t run (or runs slowly under emulation, e.g. Rosetta).
  • The ARM-that-got-away: spun out of Acorn in 1990, LSE-listed, then sold to SoftBank in 2016 for £24bn — one of the UK’s biggest tech exits. Re-floated on Nasdaq in 2023 at ~$54bn; market cap ~$150bn by 2025. Had it stayed UK-listed it would be near the very top of the FTSE — the canonical “why can’t the UK keep its tech companies” example. (Check/update the market cap before lecturing.)
  • 32- vs 64-bit Windows: “x86” came to colloquially mean the 32-bit instruction set (IA-32) and “x64” the 64-bit one — hence the Program Files (x86) folder for 32-bit apps. 64-bit Windows runs old 32-bit binaries via an emulation layer (WOW64: “Windows-on-Windows”), but not vice versa; 32-bit also caps addressable memory at 4 GB, which is why it died. (Nuance: 64-bit Windows isn’t amd64-only — Windows 11 also ships an ARM64 edition, which emulates x86/x64 apps.) Same story as amd64 generally: extend and emulate, never break old software.

Programming

Programming languages

Programming languages are the “simple”, human-facing interface to all this machinery.

What you write (C):

double mean(const double *x, int n) {
    double total = 0.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
        total += x[i];
    return total / n;
}

What the compiler turns it into (x86-64, gcc -O1):

mean:
        test    esi, esi
        jle     .L4
        mov     rax, rdi
        movsxd  rdx, esi
        lea     rdx, [rdi+rdx*8]
        pxor    xmm0, xmm0
.L3:
        addsd   xmm0, QWORD PTR [rax]
        add     rax, 8
        cmp     rax, rdx
        jne     .L3
.L2:
        pxor    xmm1, xmm1
        cvtsi2sd xmm1, esi
        divsd   xmm0, xmm1
        ret
.L4:
        pxor    xmm0, xmm0
        jmp     .L2
  • This is real output (gcc 15, -O1, Intel syntax), not pseudocode — worth demoing live on godbolt.org: change the optimisation level and watch the assembly change (at -O2/-O3 it vectorises and gets much longer).
  • Things to point at: the loop is the four lines under .L3 (add, advance pointer, compare, jump back); xmm0 is a floating-point register; the n <= 0 edge case gets its own branch (.L4).
  • One line of maths in C ≈ one instruction; the compiler handles registers, addressing, branching — that’s the “interface” doing its job.

Low-level vs high-level languages

  • The “level” of a language ≈ its distance from the machine
  • C (1972): low-level — you manage the memory, and the compiler maps your code almost 1:1 onto the hardware
  • Fortran (1957): the original high-level language — “FORmula TRANslation”, built for science; compiled and fast, with arrays as first-class citizens
  • Python (1991): very high level — no types to declare, memory managed for you, everything decided at runtime… which is exactly why it’s slow
/* the max of some numbers, in C */
double *xs = malloc(5 * sizeof(double));
if (xs == NULL)                /* did we even get the memory? */
    exit(1);
xs[0] = 1.0; xs[1] = 4.5; xs[2] = 2.0;
xs[3] = 8.1; xs[4] = 3.0;

double largest = xs[0];
for (int i = 1; i < 5; i++)
    if (xs[i] > largest)
        largest = xs[i];

free(xs);                      /* give it back, or leak it */
! the max of some numbers, in Fortran
real :: xs(5) = [1.0, 4.5, 2.0, 8.1, 3.0]
real :: largest
largest = maxval(xs)   ! arrays built in
# the max of some numbers, in Python
largest = max([1.0, 4.5, 2.0, 8.1, 3.0])

Software usage on ARCHER2, the UK national supercomputer (June 2026)

Data: ARCHER2 usage data, EPCC

  • The chart motivates “Fortran isn’t dead”: VASP (~18% of all machine time), the Met Office Unified Model, CP2K, and further down Quantum Espresso, CASTEP, NEMO, FHI-aims — Fortran codes, decades old, still eating a huge share of a 2026 national supercomputer.
  • Python is itself #6 (~5%) — but mostly as the driver: orchestrating, analysing, gluing. The heavy numerics underneath is compiled code.
  • OpenFOAM, GROMACS, LAMMPS: C++ — the other compiled contingent.
  • Nobody chooses a level in the abstract: you trade programmer time against machine time. The rest of this course lives happily at the Python level — while understanding what’s underneath.

A programming language is a contract

  • The language is abstract: syntax and rules of meaning, written down in a standard, e.g., the C and Fortran standards, the Python language reference. No machine is mentioned
  • The implementation honours that contract on a particular machine: gcc/clang for C, gfortran/flang for Fortran, CPython/PyPy for Python
  • The mean() you just saw compiles to different assembly on ARM but the C doesn’t change. That’s what portable means: write to the contract, not to the machine
  • Fresh example of a breach: this course’s own Fortran Mandelbrot kernel is standard-conforming 2018 (do concurrent + local()), and current gfortran miscompiles it — segfault. The contract was fine; the implementation wasn’t.
  • The C signed-overflow demo coming up is the other side: undefined behaviour is a hole the contract deliberately leaves open, and the implementation may do anything.
  • Python’s contract is fuzzier: the language reference exists, but CPython is the de-facto spec — which is why PyPy compatibility is a moving target.
  • And like all contracts, it has fine print: “undefined behaviour” (our overflow, later), implementation quirks, and the occasional breach a compiler bug

Everything is an abstraction

        your analysis script
              Python
      NumPy / compiled libraries
          compiler ・ OS
        assembly ・ x86, ARM
        CPU ・ gates ・ transistors
              electrons
  • Computing is a tower of abstractions — nobody holds it all in their head at once
  • The working rule: know enough to reason one layer down, and to design for the layer above
  • But abstractions leak — the Law of Leaky Abstractions: the layer below pokes through exactly when things go wrong
  • 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3, “why is this loop slow?”, “why won’t this install on my machine?”
  • Spolsky’s essay (2002) is the canonical reference: “all non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky.”
  • Good leak examples for this audience: floating point (coming up in two slides), Python’s speed (later today), loop order / cache effects in NumPy, and every environment/installation problem they will ever have.
  • The inverse skill matters too: when you write code, you are building an abstraction for someone else — usually future you.

Data structures

Data structures: bits and integers

Everything in memory is bits — a type is a rule for reading them.

The simplest type: a boolean, one bit of information, true or false:

bool done = false;
0

A 32-bit signed integer:

int n = 42;    /* exactly 4 bytes */
00000000 00000000 00000000 00101010

Range: \(-2^{31}\) to \(2^{31}-1\) = 2,147,483,647 — one more and it overflows:

int n = 2147483647 + 1;
printf("n = %d\n", n);
n = -2147483648
unsigned int u = 2147483648 + 1;
printf("u = %u\n", u);
u = 2147483649

The same 32 bits, read as a float:

float x = 42.0f;   /* also 4 bytes */
01000010 00101000 00000000 00000000
[ sign | 8-bit exponent | 23-bit fraction ]

Range: \(\pm 3.4 \times 10^{38}\) (smallest positive \(\approx 1.2 \times 10^{-38}\)) — but only ~7 significant digits

  • Y2038: signed 32-bit Unix time (number of seconds since 01/01/1970) runs out at 03:14:07 UTC on 19 January 2038
  • The demo is real gcc output. Nuance if asked: gcc does warn here (-Woverflow, because it’s a compile-time constant) and then runs it anyway; overflow from runtime values usually gets no warning at all. Signed overflow is technically undefined behaviour in C — wrap-around to the most negative value is just what x86 happens to do.
  • More overflow stories, pick to taste: Ariane 5, Gangnam Style overflowing YouTube’s 32-bit view counter (2014, forced the move to 64-bit); the Boeing 787 directive to reboot before 248 days of uptime (a centisecond counter overflows).
  • Y2038 detail: Unix time = seconds since 1 Jan 1970 in a signed 32-bit int. Desktop OSes have moved to 64-bit time, but embedded systems (cars, instruments, industrial control) haven’t — and it already bites any code doing forward-dated arithmetic (mortgages, pensions).
  • Ariane 5 detail worth telling: the overflowing code was reused from Ariane 4, where the value physically couldn’t overflow. The rocket changed; the assumption didn’t. (Ties to the living-ecosystem slide.)
  • Contrast for later: Python ints never overflow — arbitrary precision — one more thing the wardrobe hides.

Data structures: floating point (double precision)

A double is 64 bits read as \(\pm\, m \times 2^{e}\) (IEEE 754); used extensively in scientific computing.

[ 1 sign bit | 11 exponent bits | 52 fraction bits ]
double x = 0.1;               /* stored: 0.10000000000000000555… */
printf("%.17f\n", 0.1 + 0.2);   /* prints 0.30000000000000004 */
  • 0.1 has no exact binary representation — like 1/3 in decimal
  • so never compare floats with ==; test fabs(a - b) < tol
  • smaller floats (32-, 16-bit) trade precision for speed — the currency of GPUs and machine learning

Arrays and vectors

double xs[1024];              /* C: one contiguous block, size fixed forever */
std::vector<double> ys = {1.0, 4.5};  // C++: contiguous but growable
ys.push_back(2.0);   // may silently reallocate-and-copy the whole thing
  • Contiguity is why arrays are fast: caches and vector units love them

Python

The locker and the wardrobe

A C variable is a locker:

int x = 42;      /* 4 bytes at a fixed address */
x = "hello";     /* compile error: wrong shape */
  • fixed size, fixed shape, the name is the address

A Python object is the wardrobe to Narnia:

x = 42          # a name, tied to an object
x = "hello"     # fine — retie the name
  • looks like a small box, but it’s bigger on the inside: the object carries its type, its size, its reference count…

Image: another Nano Banana hallucination

Why “Python is slow”

  • Every value is an object on the heap — even 1 (a C int is 4 bytes; a Python int is 28)
  • Everything is mutable, so the interpreter can assume nothing, so cannot optimise anything ahead of time
  • Types are checked at runtime, on every operation, every time
  • A list is an array of pointers to scattered objects, no cache locality

…so why does everyone use it?

  • Clean syntax: executable pseudocode, optimised for reading
  • Hides complexity: memory management, types, compilation, linking, installation
  • The ecosystem: “batteries included” standard library, plus a mature external library for almost anything (PyPI)
  • Offloading: NumPy, PyTorch & friends are compiled C/C++/Fortran underneath
  • Economics: researcher time usually costs more than CPU time

Higher-level structures: the dictionary

In C, you build it yourself:

struct entry {
    const char *key;
    double value;
    struct entry *next;
};
unsigned hash(const char *s);   /* you write this */
double lookup(struct entry **t,
              const char *key); /* and this */
/* …and collisions, resizing, deletion,
   and who owns all that memory? */

In Fortran: no dictionary in the standard library at all.

In Python, it’s a literal:

masses = {"H": 1.008, "He": 4.003}
masses["Li"] = 6.94

Python is built on dicts: module namespaces, object attributes, keyword arguments — dicts all the way down.

Dicts speak JSON

A Python dict…

sample = {
    "formula": "NaCl",
    "mass_g": 0.253,
    "conductivity": [1.2, 1.4, 1.1],
}

import json
json.dump(sample, open("sample.json", "w"))

…is one line away from a file anything can read:

{
  "formula": "NaCl",
  "mass_g": 0.253,
  "conductivity": [1.2, 1.4, 1.1]
}
  • JSON is just dicts, lists, numbers, strings and booleans — the lingua franca of data exchange
  • Generic formats outlive any one program, language, or PhD: this file is readable from Python, R, Julia, C++, a browser, a database — today and in twenty years
  • Your instrument’s proprietary binary format is none of those things
  • Rule of thumb: keep open, generic formats (JSON, CSV, HDF5, …) at the boundaries of everything you write
  • This is the quiet superpower of dict-shaped thinking: structure your data as nested dicts/lists and it serialises for free.
  • JSON caveats worth saying out loud: no comments, floats only (no NaN in strict JSON), and it’s inefficient for big numerical arrays — that’s when you reach for HDF5/NetCDF/Parquet instead.
  • Ties directly to research data management (FAIR data) — and to every web API a student will ever call: they all speak JSON.

Example: Periodic Table, four ways

  1. A custom class
class Element:
    def __init__(self, symbol, Z, mass, name):
        self.symbol = symbol
        self.Z = Z
        self.mass = mass
        self.name = name

ptable = {
    "H":  Element("H", 1, 1.008, "hydrogen"),
    "He": Element("He", 2, 4.003, "helium"),
}
  • The progression is the point: each structure answers a question the previous one couldn’t. Variables: fine for three constants, dead end for a dataset. List: order and iteration, but the “key” is an index you have to arithmetic on (the off-by-one is a real bug class). Dict: the key becomes the thing you actually know — the symbol.
  • The nested dict is one json.dump away from a data file — two slides ahead.

Control flow in Python

Loops (for, while), conditionals (if, elif, else) and functions build programs

ptable = {
    "H": Element("H", 1, 1.008, "hydrogen"),
    "He": Element("He", 2, 4.003, "helium"),
    ...
}

def is_element(symbol):
    """Is the symbol a known element?"""
    if symbol in ptable:
        return True
    return False

is_element("H")  # True
is_element("Xx") # False
is_element(8) # False

Scientific Python

Nearly all of it stands on one shared abstraction: the NumPy array.

  • NumPy — the n-dimensional array: memory laid out like Fortran, driven from Python
  • SciPy — the numerical toolbox: integration, optimisation, linear algebra, statistics, signal processing
  • matplotlib — plotting (most of the figures in most of the papers you’ll read)
  • pandas — labelled tables (“dataframes”) for messy real-world data
  • Jupyter — notebooks: code, figures and narrative in one document
  • scikit-learn — classical machine learning; PyTorch / JAX at the deep-learning end
  • …and domain layers on top: astropy (astronomy), ASE / pymatgen (materials), RDKit (molecules), Biopython, MDAnalysis, …
  • The shared array is what makes the stack composable: a pandas column, a scikit-learn input, and a matplotlib series are all NumPy arrays underneath — everything interoperates.
  • All community-built, open source — the living ecosystem from a few slides ago, working in your favour.
  • Advice: before writing your own file parser or fitting routine, check whether your field’s domain package already has it. It usually does.

NumPy: putting the speed back

Remember why arrays are fast — one contiguous block. NumPy brings that to Python:

import numpy as np

measurements = [[1.2, 1.4, 1.1],     # a nested list of Python objects…
                [0.9, 1.3, 1.5]]
arr = np.array(measurements)         # …becomes one contiguous block of float64
2 * arr             # array([[2.4, 2.8, 2.2],
                    #        [1.8, 2.6, 3. ]])
arr.mean()          # 1.2333333333333332
arr.max(axis=1)     # array([1.4, 1.5]) — the max of each row

One line of Python = one compiled loop over the whole block

arr[0]              # first row
arr[:, 2]           # third column: array([1.1, 1.5])
arr[arr > 1.2]      # by condition:  array([1.4, 1.3, 1.5])

Slicing and masking replace most of the loops you’d otherwise write

  • This cashes in the arrays/vectors slide: same contiguous memory as C/Fortran (it is C underneath), driven from Python. The wardrobe overhead is paid once per array, not once per number.
  • The loop still happens — but in compiled code, over the whole block, with the types checked once. That’s the whole “why Python survives being slow” trick in one object.
  • The mask arr[arr > 1.2] is the same idea the NumPy Mandelbrot uses at scale (coming up).
  • All outputs shown are real (numpy prints 3. for 3.0 — floats, again).

Optional final demo: Mandelbrot

The Mandelbrot set

Iterate one very simple map:

\[z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c, \qquad z_0 = 0\]

for a complex number \(c\) — i.e. a point in the plane.

  • The set is every \(c\) for which \(z_n\) stays bounded forever
  • We can’t iterate forever, but there’s an escape test: once \(|z_n| > 2\), the orbit is gone for good — so iterate each pixel’s \(c\) up to max_iter times and colour it by when it escaped
  • The boundary is a fractal: infinitely detailed, new structure at every zoom
  • And it’s a perfect benchmark: millions of pixels × hundreds of iterations of pure arithmetic, nothing to hide behind

  • Named for Benoît Mandelbrot, who first plotted it at IBM in 1980 — a computational discovery: nobody had seen it before computers, despite the underlying maths (Julia, Fatou) dating to ~1918.
  • Why 2? If \(|z_n|\) ever exceeds 2, the \(z^2\) term dominates and the orbit provably diverges — that’s what makes a finite test correct, not a heuristic.
  • Black pixels = never escaped within max_iter = (approximately) in the set; the colour bands are escape times.

Demo: Mandelbrot

Pure Python:

x = y = 0.0
n = 0
while (x*x + y*y < 4.0
       and n < max_iter):
    x, y = (x*x - y*y + cx,
            2.0*x*y + cy)
    n += 1
out[j][i] = n

NumPy (whole grid at once):

for n in range(max_iter):
    z[mask] = z[mask]**2 + c[mask]
    escaped = mask & (np.abs(z) > 2)
    out[escaped] = n
    mask &= ~escaped

Fortran (via f2py):

do while (x*x + y*y < 4.0d0 &
          .and. n < max_iter)
    xt = x*x - y*y + cx
    y  = 2.0d0*x*y + cy
    x  = xt
    n  = n + 1
end do

Wrap-up

Recap

  • A century of hardware, one enduring idea: the stored program, fetch–decode–execute — everything else is miniaturisation
  • Languages are abstractions over that machine: compiled ones (C, Fortran) stay close to it; Python trades machine time for your time
  • Types are rules for reading bits — and every abstraction leaks (overflow, 0.1 + 0.2, “why is this slow?”)
  • Python hides the machinery in objects and dicts; when you need speed, offload to the compiled layer (NumPy) — or fix the algorithm first

Not covered

  • Randomness: pseudo-random numbers, seeds, and why your “random” results should be reproducible
  • Recursion: functions that call themselves
  • Classes and object-oriented design — you’ll meet them the moment you use a library
  • Parallelism: threads, processes, MPI, GPUs
  • Exceptions and error handling
  • Algorithmic complexity, properly (big-O beyond today’s hand-waving)
  • Text encodings, regular expressions, databases, the network…
  • The point of this slide: students leave knowing these exist and are learnable, rather than discovering them mid-crisis.
  • Randomness and reproducibility is a good candidate to pull forward into a practical (seeding numpy, why “it worked yesterday”).

Software is a living ecosystem

  • Everything you’ve seen today changes: languages, libraries, hardware — software grows organically around historical biases (x86’s baggage, Fortran’s arrays, Python’s dicts)
  • You are part of the ecosystem: the code you write and the tools you choose feed back into it
  • So the same code will not always work, everywhere, forever — unless you fence it in: pinned dependencies, recorded environments, containerisation
